# Principle 3: Verifying that sources and scripts exist

## What the principle addresses

Two failures share one shape. In the first, a claim is attributed to a source that does not exist: a paper with a plausible title, plausible authors and a real journal name, a DOI that resolves to nothing, a quotation nobody said. In the second, a procedure is described in terms of a file, script, command or package that is not there: `scripts/clean_data.py` that was never written, a command-line flag that was removed two versions ago, a library that was never published. In both cases the artifact is referenced in exactly the surface form of something real. A citation has the shape of a citation and a path has the shape of a path, and it is the surface form that a reader trusts or an execution environment acts on.

The principle asks for a check before that acting: does the referent exist, and can it be reached? That is narrower than "is the claim true". A source can exist and fail to support the sentence it is attached to. A script can exist and be the wrong script. The evidence below shows the narrow check is necessary but not sufficient, so this report treats the principle as three separate tests: existence, availability at the point of use, and support.

One further distinction runs through everything. There is the check a model performs on itself, by re-reading its own output, and the check a system performs on the model, by consulting the world. The first is unreliable in a way that is now fairly well documented. The second is ordinary engineering.

## Established frameworks and research

The measurement literature on fabricated references is the place to start, because it establishes the base rate. Walters and Wilder asked GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to write 84 short literature reviews across 42 topics, then attempted to locate all 636 citations in bibliographic databases and on the web. Fifty-five per cent of the GPT-3.5 citations and 18 per cent of the GPT-4 citations referred to works that did not exist. Of the citations that did point to real works, 43 per cent of the GPT-3.5 set and 24 per cent of the GPT-4 set contained substantive errors in the bibliographic details [2]. Two details matter for design. The fabrications were not random strings; most carried the names of real journals, publishers and organisations, which is what makes them persuasive. The paper also notes, drawing on earlier work, that ChatGPT often answers inaccurately when asked to verify the legitimacy of the works it cites [2]. The study measures the failure; it does not test a remedy.

The evaluation community has a name for the property being violated. Attributable to Identified Sources requires that output pertaining to the external world be verified against an independent, provided source, and supplies a two-stage annotation pipeline for deciding whether a claim is fully supported, partially supported or unsupported by that source [1]. It is a normative evaluation framework rather than an empirical finding, and its validation consists of showing that annotators can apply it consistently across conversational question answering, summarisation and table-to-text data [1]. Its contribution here is definitional. It makes "the source exists" a separate question from "the source supports the sentence", and treats only the second as attribution.

At system scale, two benchmarks make both properties measurable. ALCE builds an end-to-end pipeline that must retrieve evidence and cite it, with automatic metrics for fluency, correctness and citation quality that the authors show correlate with human judgement; on the ELI5 dataset even the best systems lacked complete citation support 50 per cent of the time [3]. FActScore takes the complementary route of decomposing a generation into atomic facts and computing the share supported by a reliable knowledge source. ChatGPT scored 58 per cent on biographies of people, and the authors show an automated estimator can track the human score with less than a two per cent error rate [4]. Neither paper is a finding about a deployed product, but together they establish that citation support and factual precision are measurable quantities, and that neither is close to saturated.

For the artifact half of the principle, the clearest evidence concerns software packages. Spracklen and colleagues generated 576,000 code samples with 16 code-generating models and checked every recommended package against the real registries. At least 5.2 per cent of the packages recommended by commercial models and 21.7 per cent of those recommended by open-source models did not exist, across 205,474 distinct invented names [5]. The paper names the resulting attack: an adversary watches which non-existent names a model tends to produce and publishes malicious packages under those names, so that the next developer who trusts the suggestion installs the attacker's code. The authors report that several mitigation strategies substantially reduce the rate without hurting code quality [5]. This is the strongest available evidence that models routinely name artifacts that are not present, and that the name is acted on.

Code-level work supplies the taxonomy. CodeHalu defines code hallucination as code that is syntactically correct or semantically plausible but does not execute as expected or meet the requirement, and sorts it into mapping, naming, resource and logic hallucinations [6]. Two subcategories are this principle exactly: external source hallucinations, where generated code imports a module that does not exist or loads one from the wrong path, and identity hallucinations, where code references undefined variables or non-existent object properties [6]. The benchmark covers 699 tasks and 8,883 samples across 17 models. The finding that makes the principle urgent is the base rate of plausibility. Across 105,958 samples from 16 models, only nine models occasionally produced syntax errors at all, at an average rate of 0.0020 [6]. Code that looks right is the normal case, so appearance carries almost no information about whether the thing it names exists.

Existence is also time-dependent. Klein and colleagues tested more than one million references to web resources extracted from over 3.5 million science, technology and medicine articles published between 1997 and 2012, polling each URI on the live web and checking web archives for a snapshot representative of the referenced state. One in five articles suffered from reference rot, the combination of link rot and content drift that makes the cited context impossible to revisit, rising to seven in ten among articles that cite web resources at all [7]. A citation verified on the day it is written can stop resolving later, and a link that still resolves can show different content.

On how to check, two papers pull in different directions and together mark out a boundary. Chain-of-Verification has a model draft an answer, plan verification questions, answer those questions independently so that the answers are not biased by the draft, and then produce a revised response; it reports fewer hallucinations on list-based Wikidata questions, closed-book MultiSpanQA and long-form generation [8]. Huang and colleagues examine intrinsic self-correction, where the model tries to repair its own answer using only its own capabilities and no external feedback, and find that on reasoning tasks models struggle to do this and sometimes perform worse after self-correction [9]. Read together, the results suggest that a verification step helps when it is structured to generate independent evidence, and does not help when it is the same model re-reading the same draft. Neither paper tests verification against an external corpus or a file system, so applying them to agent design is an extension rather than a direct result.

The normative layer is thinner but explicit. NIST's Generative AI Profile defines confabulation to include outputs that "may also include confabulated logic or citations that purport to justify or explain the system's answer", and notes that such material may further mislead humans into inappropriately trusting the output [10]. Among its suggested actions, MS-2.5-003 tells organisations to "review and verify sources and citations in GAI system outputs during pre-deployment risk measurement and ongoing monitoring activities", and MS-2.5-005 tells them to verify data provenance and that retrieval-augmented generation data is grounded [10]. These are recommendations inside a voluntary framework, not evidence that the practice works. OWASP's entry on misinformation is similar in kind and more concrete about failure modes: it lists fabricated legal cases, non-existent or insecure code libraries, and overreliance, meaning users failing to verify output, and recommends retrieval-augmented generation, cross-checking against trusted sources and automated validation of key outputs [11].

The engineering tradition supplies the sharpest formal statement of the artifact half. CWE-367, time-of-check time-of-use, describes the class of defect in which a resource is checked and then used, with its state able to change in between. The canonical example checks a file's status and then updates the file, and the entry observes that the file could have changed between the check and the update [12]. The lesson transfers directly: "the script exists" is a fact about the moment of checking, not about the moment of execution.

## Strength of evidence

The measurement findings are strong on their own terms. Fabricated references have been measured with transparent methods and clear samples [2], and the package result comes from a peer-reviewed security venue with 576,000 samples and a distinguished paper award [5]. Reference rot rests on a corpus of more than a million references [7]. None of this is seriously contested.

What is weaker is currency and transfer. The citation rates come from 2023 models. The gap between 55 per cent and 18 per cent within a single model generation shows that the number is a property of the model rather than of the technology, so it should be read as evidence that fabrication is endemic, not as an estimate for any system in use today. The package study measures single-turn code generation, not what an agent does after an install fails. I found no study that measures how often agents reference files or scripts that do not exist in real trajectories, and none that measures whether requiring a pre-execution existence check reduces incidents. That part of the principle is extrapolated from code benchmarks and from a security taxonomy, which is a reasonable inference but not a measured effect.

The verification literature is emerging and partly contested. CoVe reports reductions on benchmark tasks [8]; Huang and colleagues report that intrinsic self-correction does not reliably help and can hurt [9]. The two are reconcilable if the effective ingredient is independence of the verification evidence rather than the act of asking again, but that reconciliation is an interpretation, not a result either paper reports. I found no field experiment on verification gates in deployed agents.

The governance material is normative throughout. NIST's actions are suggestions in a voluntary framework [10], OWASP is practitioner guidance [11], and CWE is a taxonomy of weakness classes rather than a study of their frequency [12]. AIS is an evaluation standard whose validity rests on annotator agreement, not on predictive validity for harm [1]. These documents are good evidence of what serious institutions expect and no evidence that the expectation is met.

One real disagreement concerns trust rather than existence. The verification literature treats a supported citation as an improvement, while OWASP treats visible citations as a possible amplifier of overreliance [11], and NIST observes that confident presentation increases the chance that users believe false content [10]. Both can be true at once. A citation raises credibility whether or not anyone checks it, which is precisely why an unchecked citation is worse than no citation.

## Practical implementation

Resolve references with a tool at the point of use, never from memory. In practice that means fetching the URL and recording the status code, stating the path, querying the registry, or running the command's help output. A model's confidence that a module exists is not evidence, because models produce plausible names at rates measured in the single digits to tens of per cent [5] and produce syntactically clean code almost always [6].

Return three states rather than two. A reference is confirmed when it resolves and supports the claim, present but unsupported when it resolves and does not back the sentence, and unresolved when it could not be reached. AIS supplies the standard for the middle state [1], and the citation-error rates show that the middle state is common rather than exotic [2]. Collapsing unresolved into false is also a mistake, because paywalls and regional blocks produce unreachable but genuine sources.

Bind the check to the use. For files, open once and operate on the handle rather than checking a path and reopening it later. For packages, pin a version and verify the lockfile hash at install. For long-running work, re-verify after any wait or handoff. This is the direct application of CWE-367 [12], and it is the part most often skipped, because the check and the use usually happen in different tool calls.

Verify code by execution, not inspection. Run the script in a sandbox with a minimal smoke test, capture the exit status and standard error, and report both. Treat "it parses" as close to no information, given the 0.0020 syntax error rate across 105,958 samples [6]. When execution is impossible, say the script is unexecuted rather than implying that it works.

Keep an artifact manifest for the run. Every path, command, identifier or package named in the final answer should correspond to an entry created by an actual tool result: the path returned by the write call, the version string returned by the resolver, the exit code from the run. Anything absent from the manifest is labelled as proposed. This is a design choice rather than a finding, but it follows from the observation that the model's generated reference and the environment's real object are different things that look identical in text.

Control the namespace instead of trusting the name. Restrict installs to an allowlisted registry, prefer pinned and already-present dependencies, and never auto-install a package name that the model produced in the same turn. Spracklen and colleagues show the attack works by populating exactly those invented names, and report that mitigation strategies reduce the rate [5]; OWASP lists non-existent or insecure libraries as a recognised failure mode [11].

Measure the property instead of asserting it. Two instruments are available off the shelf: atomic-fact support in the style of FActScore [4] and citation quality in the style of ALCE [3]. Run them on a sample of outputs as a regression test, and track the rate of unresolved references and corrected claims over time. NIST's MS-2.5-003 places source and citation verification in pre-deployment measurement and ongoing monitoring, which is the right cadence for a property that decays [10].

Give the verifier the evidence, and separate it from the writer. A second pass that re-reads the draft without new information is the setting in which self-correction has been shown not to help [9]. A pass that generates specific verification questions and answers them independently is the setting that has shown gains [8]. The strongest version, and the one AIS formalises, uses an independent judge with the source in hand [1].

Preserve what was verified. When a claim depends on a web source, store the retrieved text, a hash or the archive URL alongside the citation. Reference rot means the link that worked at verification time may not work at reading time [7], and an archived snapshot is the only way to keep the claim checkable later.

Fail visibly. When a reference cannot be resolved, the useful output is the claim, its status and what would resolve it, not a substitute source that happens to be reachable. Substitution is the mechanism that converts a missing citation into a false one.

## Tensions and limits

Existence is not legitimacy. The package-hallucination attack works because the attacker makes the missing name exist [5]. A check that asks only whether the name resolves passes exactly when the adversary has done their job. Verification therefore needs a provenance question alongside it: who published this, when, and is it the artifact the user meant. The same holds for sources, where a real paper can be retracted, predatory, or about a different population than the claim requires.

Existence is not support. AIS separates the two [1] and the citation-error rates show why [2]. An agent that checks only resolvability will attach real but irrelevant sources, which is harder for a reader to catch than an obviously broken link.

Verification costs coverage and latency. If the rule is that nothing may be stated without a resolved source, the ALCE result of best systems lacking complete support half the time [3] suggests that a strict rule would suppress a large share of otherwise useful output. The defensible compromise is proportional: full verification for load-bearing claims and for anything executed, explicit uncertainty for the rest. That is a judgment, not something the literature settles.

Access is unequal. Paywalls, login walls and regional blocks make real sources unreachable. A rule that equates "cannot open" with "does not exist" penalises users without institutional subscriptions and pushes the agent toward substituting an open-access paper that says something different. Report access status; do not convert it into a truth claim.

Verification is itself an action with side effects. Fetching a URL discloses interest to the server, may load tracking pixels, can reach internal addresses the user did not intend to expose, and pulls untrusted content into the agent's context. Some checks deserve the same gating as any other outbound request. Verification also consumes the budget that would otherwise go to the task, which is the direct tension with helpfulness.

A verification record can produce false assurance. A green check, a listed citation or a "verified" badge raises credibility whether or not the underlying check meant anything [10] [11]. If verification is automated and unreviewed, it can convert an unverified claim into an apparently verified one, which is worse than leaving it unmarked.

User control cuts both ways. A user may ask for a citation to a document the agent cannot access, or may prefer speed to checking. Silently complying produces a fabricated reference; silently refusing overrides the user. The defensible behaviour is to perform the check, report the result and let the user decide, which leaves the decision with the person who bears the consequence.

The residual limit is structural. Checking and using are different events, and no amount of checking at one moment guarantees the state at the next [12]. For artifacts that matter, the only complete answer is to make use atomic, by holding the handle, pinning the hash and running in a sealed environment, rather than to check harder.

The evidence is thinnest on agent trajectories. The literature measures model outputs, not the sequence of claims an agent makes about its own workspace. The failure modes of citing a file path that was never created, or reporting tests that were never run, are widely reported by practitioners but, as far as I could verify, not quantified in peer-reviewed work. That is the gap a serious evaluation would fill, and until it is filled the artifact half of this principle rests on analogy from code generation and from security engineering.
