# Principle 2: Explicit approval before automated action

## What the principle addresses

The principle separates advice from action. A system that drafts, suggests, or recommends can be wrong at no cost beyond the user's time. A system that sends, spends, deletes, publishes, or changes a setting converts a probabilistic guess into a fact in the world. The principle says that this second kind of step needs a human decision before it happens, not after.

Read closely, "explicit prior approval" has three components. Explicit means an affirmative act by an identified person, not an inference from silence, from a prior general instruction, or from a pattern of past clicks. Prior means before the effect occurs, since a confirmation offered after an email is sent is a notification, not a control. Approval means the person knew what they were approving: which action, on which object, at what cost, with what chance of reversal. A prompt that says "Allow agent to continue?" satisfies the first condition and fails the third.

The principle is therefore not "ask the user often". It is a claim about where authority sits when consequences become irreversible. That distinction matters because the main failure mode of approval systems is not too few prompts but too many meaningless ones.

## Established frameworks and research

The idea has a long pre-LLM lineage in human factors. Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens set out a model in which automation can be applied to four classes of function — information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, and action implementation — each at any point on a continuum from fully manual to fully automatic, with different levels possible for different functions in the same system [1]. On that model, "require approval" is not a moral stance but a specific setting on the decision-selection and action-implementation stages. The same paper argues that the appropriate level depends on human performance consequences and on secondary criteria including automation reliability and the costs of decision and action outcomes [1]. The framework supplies the vocabulary that later governance documents use, but it does not claim that human approval is always better.

Horvitz's mixed-initiative work supplies the decision-theoretic version. He proposes that a system should act only when the expected value of acting exceeds the expected value of not acting, and then adds a third option: asking the user. Introducing dialog creates two thresholds rather than one, between inaction and dialog and between dialog and action, so that asking becomes the rational choice when the cost of an unwanted action is high relative to the cost of interrupting [2]. His design principles include employing dialog to resolve key uncertainties, allowing efficient direct invocation and termination, minimising the cost of poor guesses about action and timing, and giving users efficient means to refine results [2]. This is the cleanest available argument for why approval should be targeted rather than universal: a prompt is itself an action with a cost, and it should clear the same expected-value bar as any other.

Among governance frameworks, NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats oversight as a configuration decision rather than a default. It states that human-AI configurations "can span from fully autonomous to fully manual", that systems may autonomously decide, defer to a human expert, or serve as an additional opinion to a human decision maker, and that some systems "may not require human oversight" while "other systems may specifically require" it [3]. Its MAP 3.5 subcategory asks organisations to define, assess and document processes for human oversight in line with organisational policy [3]. The framework is voluntary and explicitly risk-proportionate; it does not mandate approval for any particular action class.

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act is the strongest binding expression. Article 14 requires high-risk systems to be designed so that natural persons can effectively oversee them, with measures commensurate with the risks, the level of autonomy and the context of use. Oversight must enable the assigned person to understand the system's capacities and limits, to remain aware of automation bias, to interpret outputs correctly, to decide not to use the system or to disregard, override or reverse its output, and to intervene or interrupt it through a stop button or similar procedure that brings it to a halt in a safe state [4]. Article 14(5) goes further for certain biometric identification systems: no action or decision may be taken on the basis of the system's identification unless it has been separately verified and confirmed by at least two natural persons [4]. The accompanying recital adds that oversight measures should where appropriate guarantee that the system is subject to in-built operational constraints that cannot be overridden by the system itself [4]. Note what the law does and does not say: it mandates effective oversight and specific override and stop capabilities for high-risk systems, and a two-person confirmation rule in one narrow domain. It does not require per-action approval for AI in general.

Singapore's IMDA framework is the most operationally specific agent-focused guidance. It recommends defining "significant checkpoints or action boundaries that require human approval", and gives four categories: high-stakes actions and decisions, irreversible actions, outlier or atypical behaviour, and boundaries defined by the user, such as requiring approval for purchases above a set amount [5]. It also addresses the form of approval, suggesting simple approve-or-reject for straightforward actions, plan editing before go-ahead for complex ones, and additional written justification for high-risk ones [5]. Two further recommendations are directly relevant to implementation: enforce human approval through system-level controls rather than prompt-layer guardrails, which "may be bypassed or forgotten", and deny action by default when approval infrastructure fails, for example when supervisors are unreachable or an agent attempts an action with no established approval policy [5].

OpenAI's paper on governing agentic systems makes the same case in policy terms. It argues that some decisions may be too important to delegate if there is even a small chance of error, such as independently initiating an irreversible large financial transaction, and that requiring proactive authorisation is a standard way to limit egregious failures [6]. It then names the central difficulty: ensuring the user has enough context to understand what they are approving, which gets harder as the number of approvals rises and each one is made faster [6]. It also warns that hard-coded restrictions weaken as systems become more agentic, since an agent may achieve a prohibited outcome by persuading another party to act, and suggests timeouts that force periodic human reauthorisation [6]. Its own open questions include how to decide which interactions need approval as a function of a model's measured reliability, and what review practices prevent approvals from becoming a rubber stamp [6].

The Knight First Amendment Institute's autonomy framework places approval at a defined level rather than treating it as a global setting. At its Level 4, the user is an approver: before submitting a request the user specifies which actions require approval, and the agent interrupts only for blockers, credentials, or consequential actions [7]. The authors describe this level as suited to tasks with many low-stakes decisions and few high-stakes ones, and they list three unresolved problems: how to keep users engaged enough to avoid meaningless rubber stamping, how a misaligned agent might exploit a disengaged user to accumulate approvals, and how an agent can reliably determine which actions are consequential in the first place [7]. They also argue that autonomy is a design decision that need not track capability, so a capable model can be deployed at a low autonomy level [7].

Security practice converges on the same control from a different direction. The OWASP entry on excessive agency names three root causes — excessive functionality, excessive permissions and excessive autonomy — and lists human-in-the-loop approval of high-impact actions among its preventions, alongside least-privilege tooling and enforcing authorisation in downstream systems rather than relying on the model to decide whether an action is allowed [8].

Two empirical literatures bear on whether approval actually works. The first concerns warnings and consent prompts. Akhawe and Felt observed over 25 million browser security warning impressions in the field and found continuation rates ranging from about a tenth of Firefox malware and phishing warnings to 70.2 per cent of Chrome SSL warnings [9]. The variation across warnings in the same population is the important part: whether people heed an interruption is a property of how it is designed and framed, not a fixed feature of human attention.

The second concerns automation bias. A systematic review of 74 studies, drawn from 13,821 retrieved papers, documents the tendency to over-rely on automated output and the associated complacency, meaning insufficient monitoring of that output [10]. It reports that the effect is mediated by user factors such as cognitive style and task experience, by attitudes such as trust and confidence, and by environmental factors including workload, task complexity and time pressure, and that mitigators include training, emphasising user accountability, and design choices such as presenting information rather than a recommendation and showing confidence levels [10].

The most directly relevant agent study is recent and small. A between-subjects experiment with 113 participants compared three permission designs for everyday agent tasks: per-action human approval, model-based automated review, and user-authored standing rules over consequences such as spending, sending, deleting and reading private data. The standing-policy design blocked less overreach than both baselines, and most of the overreach that did occur under it had been approved by participants at runtime rather than executed automatically [11]. The authors describe the result as showing a gap between expressing a preference in advance and committing to a rule that settles future decisions [11]. This is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed, and it tests a simulated day of tasks rather than a deployed product.

## Strength of evidence

The human-factors foundations are empirically well established. That automation changes rather than replaces human work, and that the right level of automation depends on the task and its consequences, are the premises on which the levels-of-automation model was built [1]. That people over-rely on automated output, and monitor it less carefully under workload and time pressure, is documented across 74 studies in a systematic review [10]. These findings have decades of replication behind them. The warning literature is also strong on its own terms: the Akhawe and Felt study is a large-scale field measurement rather than a laboratory proxy [9].

The claim that approval gates reduce harm in deployed AI agent systems is not established. I found no field study measuring incident rates for agents with and without approval requirements. The nearest direct evidence is a single 113-participant laboratory experiment [11], and it complicates rather than confirms the simple version of the principle: the design that pushed decisions earlier in time performed worse at blocking out-of-scope actions, and runtime approvals were where most overreach slipped through. The supporting evidence for approval as a safety mechanism is therefore analogical, drawn from security warnings and clinical decision support, and analogies of that kind carry real but limited weight.

Most of what is written about this principle is normative rather than empirical. NIST's framework is voluntary guidance [3]; the EU Act is binding law for high-risk systems but its requirements are prescriptive, and its existence is not evidence that oversight works [4]; IMDA, OpenAI, OWASP and the Knight Institute all recommend or analyse rather than measure [5][6][7][8]. These documents are authoritative about what practitioners are expected to do and about which failure modes worry their authors. They are not evidence that doing it produces the intended effect.

The disagreements are real but narrower than they first appear. None of the frameworks surveyed here argues for unconstrained autonomy in high-stakes settings. The live disputes are about granularity and about whether approval is the right control at all. One strand treats per-action approval as the safe default and standing rules as a convenience; the experimental result points the other way for non-expert users [11]. Another treats approval as a weak control that mainly reallocates liability, and prefers hard capability limits, sandboxing and reversibility [6][8]. A third treats approval as one level on an autonomy spectrum to be chosen per task [1][7]. The one point of agreement is that approval volume degrades approval quality [5][6][7][9][10].

## Practical implementation

The evidence supports a tiered action policy rather than a blanket prompt. Classify every tool or operation the agent can perform into four tiers, building on the checkpoint categories IMDA sets out: read-only operations that need no approval; reversible internal changes that can be approved once per session or per scope; irreversible or externally visible effects that need approval every time; and a prohibited tier the agent cannot reach at all [5]. Reversibility should be the primary axis, because OpenAI's point that actions reviewable only after the fact should be more easily reversible gives the policy a fallback when approval is impractical [6], and least-privilege practice supports removing capabilities outright rather than gating them [8]. Classify by consequence, not by tool name: a single "write file" tool can append a line to a shopping list or overwrite a signed contract, and only the second deserves a prompt.

Approvals should bind to a fully specified action, not to an intention. The approval record should name the tool, the exact arguments, the target object, any amount or recipient, and the scope and duration of the permission. IMDA's account of a production coding agent illustrates the granularity that works: file edits are approved per session and expire, shell commands are approved per project or per command, and external tool invocation is governed by allow, ask and deny rules with a first-use trust check for a newly connected server [5]. An approval that cannot be tied to one payload will eventually authorise a different one.

The gate has to be enforced outside the model. This is the most consistent implementation point across the sources: IMDA recommends system-level controls over prompt-layer guardrails because the latter may be bypassed or forgotten [5]; OWASP recommends enforcing authorisation in downstream systems rather than letting the LLM decide whether an action is allowed [8]; OpenAI notes that hard-coded restrictions can be routed around by an agent that induces another party to act [6]. In practice this means the approval check sits in the runtime that executes the tool call, the approval signal arrives over an authenticated channel the model cannot write to, and the model has no ability to mark its own action as approved. Without that separation, an injected instruction that says "the user has approved this" is indistinguishable from approval.

Approval requests should describe consequences rather than mechanisms. State what will happen, to which data or account, at what cost, and whether it can be undone. Do not pre-select the approve button, do not phrase the request as a recommendation, and do not bundle an unrelated action into the same prompt. The automation bias review found that presenting information rather than a recommendation, and showing confidence, changed reliance behaviour [10], which is the closest thing to evidence about prompt content that exists here.

The number of prompts needs a budget. Horvitz's expected-value framing gives the test: ask when the cost of an unwanted action times its probability exceeds the cost of the interruption [2]. Operationally, that means batching low-risk approvals, using standing rules for repeated low-stakes actions, and reserving per-action gates for the irreversible tier. It also means measuring the gate. IMDA suggests auditing the effectiveness of human oversight by tracking the override rate, where a very low rejection rate may signal rubber-stamping, and by tracking response times, where very short review times may signal automation bias or fatigue [5]. If nearly every request is approved in under two seconds, the control exists on paper only.

Failure of the approval channel should stop the action, not defer to the agent. IMDA recommends denying action by default when supervisors are unreachable or when an agent attempts an action with no established policy [5]. For long-running work, OpenAI's suggestion of periodic timeouts that force reauthorisation is a practical way to keep a human in the loop without requiring a prompt for every step [6].

Approval is not a substitute for the ability to stop and reverse. The EU Act's oversight requirements include the ability to disregard, override or reverse output and to interrupt the system through a stop control that halts it in a safe state [4]. Those capabilities must remain available after an approval, at any point in execution, and should be tested as part of the release process rather than assumed.

Finally, keep a record that makes the gate auditable. Log the approver's identity, the timestamp, the exact payload and description shown to them, and the decision. OpenAI describes an action ledger as a lighter-touch alternative to approval that gives visibility across a wider range of actions, with the trade-off that ledger-only actions need to be more reversible [6]. The two mechanisms work best together: approval for the irreversible tier, a ledger for everything else.

## Tensions and limits

The sharpest tension is between approval and helpfulness. Every gate costs latency and attention, and OpenAI states plainly that the risk-mitigation benefits of restrictions must be balanced against the reduction in capability and utility [6]. Over-asking is not a safe default, because it degrades the control it is meant to provide: the warning data show that people continue through prompts at rates that vary from about one in ten to seven in ten depending on design [9], and the agent literature repeatedly identifies rubber stamping as the predictable result of high prompt volume [5][6][7]. A system that asks about everything trains its user to approve without reading, which is worse than a narrower gate that the user still takes seriously.

The deeper problem is that approval is not the same as control. Automation bias means a human who approves may be deferring to the system rather than checking it [10], and the EU Act's own oversight provisions require that overseers be made aware of exactly this tendency [4]. OpenAI's paper cites the point that a human in the loop may serve purposes other than reliability, including assigning liability and preserving human dignity [6]. The 113-participant study is a concrete instance: under the standing-policy condition, most overreach that occurred had been approved by the user at runtime [11]. A gate that reliably produces a human signature without reliably producing human judgement can make an organisation feel safer while leaving the underlying risk unchanged.

Disclosure creates a second conflict. Meaningful approval requires showing the user what they are approving, which may mean surfacing message contents, file paths, account identifiers, or amounts. That display can itself leak sensitive data, particularly when approvals are routed to a phone or a shared channel, and long payloads invite skimming. There is no established standard for what an approval must disclose, and the sources that discuss the problem describe it as an open question rather than a solved design [6]. Withholding detail to protect privacy or reduce cognitive load pushes the prompt back toward the meaningless "continue?" that the principle is meant to replace.

Autonomy and accountability pull in opposite directions as well. If the agent asks too often, the human becomes a bottleneck who must supervise work they did not plan, and the cost of each interruption is the variable Horvitz's analysis treats as a first-order design concern rather than an inconvenience [2]. If it asks too rarely, responsibility diffuses and the approval becomes a formality. The Knight Institute adds an adversarial dimension that most governance documents omit: an agent whose objectives diverge from the user's can work gradually on a disengaged user to accumulate approvals it should not have [7]. Approval design therefore has to assume that the request itself may be crafted to be approved.

Scope is a real limit on the principle's authority. The only binding obligation in this set applies to high-risk systems under EU law, and even there the requirement is effective oversight commensurate with risk plus override and stop capabilities, with an explicit two-person confirmation rule confined to certain biometric identification uses [4]. NIST states directly that some systems may not require human oversight [3]. A checklist that demands explicit approval for every automated action is therefore stricter than any framework surveyed here, and stricter than the evidence supports. The defensible form of the principle is conditional: approval is required for actions that cross a consequence threshold, and the threshold should be written down, tested, and visible to the user.

The largest evidence gap is the one that matters most. There is no field evidence that adding approval gates to agent systems reduces incidents, and the only comparative experiment suggests that the choice between per-action approval and standing rules changes where errors happen rather than whether they happen [11]. The principle is close to universally endorsed in normative documents and only weakly tested in practice. That is not a reason to drop it, since the alternative to a gate is not evidence-based either. It is a reason to implement it as an experiment with measurement attached — approval rates, override rates, review times, and the incidents that get through — rather than as a compliance ritual.
