# Attribution and credit in AI-assisted work

## What the principle addresses

Two claims travel together in this principle, and they are different in kind. The first is distributive: the person who supplied the ideas should be identified as their source, and should not lose that standing because a system helped develop them. The second is categorical: the system itself is an instrument, not a party with authorial standing. The first is about credit, the second about status. They are usually discussed together because a single failure produces both at once: a fluent artifact with no visible human behind it and no visible machine in front of it.

It helps to separate three things the word "author" carries. Credit is reputational attribution, the link between a work and the person who wants to be known for it. Authorship is a formal status, defined by law and by professional bodies, that carries rights and duties. Accountability is answerability for error. The frameworks examined below tie all three together, and the tie runs through accountability rather than through creativity. The ICMJE, the body that sets authorship rules for medical journals, states that chatbots "should not be listed as authors because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity, and originality of the work, and these responsibilities are required for authorship" [1]. The Committee on Publication Ethics reaches the same conclusion on the same ground: AI tools "cannot meet the requirements for authorship as they cannot take responsibility for the submitted work" [2].

That reasoning matters for implementation. If the exclusion of AI from authorship rested on creative incapacity, it would weaken every time a model produced something more original. Because it rests on the capacity to answer for the work, it holds regardless of output quality, and it implies that responsibility must land somewhere identifiable, with a person or an organisation. It also explains why the principle is not a claim that AI contributes nothing. A system can write most of a document and remain a tool in the relevant sense, because the sense at issue is normative rather than causal.

Three situations have to be distinguished, because the principle applies differently to each. In assistive use, a person writes and a system edits, restructures, or suggests; the human is the author and the obligation is to disclose the assistance. In co-creation, a person supplies direction and the system supplies the expressive substance; under US law the human may hold no copyright in the output at all, yet still be the person who commissioned and selected it, which is a form of credit that is real but weaker than authorship. In laundering, machine output is presented as human work; this is the failure the principle exists to prevent, and it is the case where credit is actively taken rather than merely allocated.

One further distinction keeps the principle honest. Copyright protects expression, not ideas, so "credit for ideas" is enforced by professional norms and reputational practice rather than by property law. The legal machinery described below governs who owns a text, not who thought of the argument in it. That gap is a limit of the principle, not a defect in its statement, and it is taken up in the final section.

## Established frameworks and research

### Scholarly publishing: authorship as accountability

Publishing has the most explicit and most convergent rules, and they are worth stating precisely because they are the clearest existing operationalisation of the principle. The ICMJE requires authors to disclose at submission whether they used AI-assisted technologies, to describe in both the cover letter and the submitted work how they used them, and to accept that humans remain responsible for any submitted material that included such tools [1]. It adds two rules that bear directly on attribution: authors "should be able to assert that there is no plagiarism in their paper, including in text and images produced by the AI", and "referencing AI-generated material as the primary source is not acceptable" [1]. Nondisclosure "may require corrective action and may be construed as misconduct in some circumstances" [1].

COPE's position statement adds the legal-capacity argument that the ICMJE leaves implicit. Because AI tools are "non-legal entities, they cannot assert the presence or absence of conflicts of interest nor manage copyright and license agreements" [2]. Authors must disclose in the methods, or a similar section, how the tool was used and which tool it was, and they remain "fully responsible for the content of their manuscript, even those parts produced by an AI tool" [2].

The significance of this body of policy is that it is settled. ICMJE, COPE, WAME and major journal networks have converged on the same three-part rule: no machine authorship, full human accountability, specific disclosure. This is a professional norm with enforcement behind it, not a proposal. It is also entirely normative. Nothing in these documents reports a measurement; they state what ought to be done and why.

### Copyright: authorship as human creativity

Copyright law reaches the same conclusion from a different premise. The US Copyright Office's 2025 report on copyrightability concludes that "the use of AI tools to assist rather than stand in for human creativity does not affect the availability of copyright protection for the output", that "copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements", and that "based on the functioning of current generally available technology, prompts do not alone provide sufficient control" [3]. Protection can attach to human expression that is perceptible in an AI output, to the creative selection, coordination or arrangement of material, and to creative modifications of it [3].

Two details in that report matter more than the headline. First, the Office draws the line by use rather than by technology: "there is an important distinction between using AI as a tool to assist in the creation of works and using AI as a stand-in for human creativity", and "this distinction depends on how the system is being used, not on its inherent characteristics" [3]. Second, it separates the copyright in a prompt from the copyright in an output. Prompts "if sufficiently creative, may be copyrightable", but that is "a separate question" from the status of the output, and most commenters agreed that simple prompts do not make a user the author of what the system produced [3].

The D.C. Circuit's decision in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* supplies the statutory reasoning. The court held that the Copyright Act "requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being", and that the Act's provisions "define 'machines' as tools used by humans in the creative process rather than as creators themselves" [4]. The premise here is textual and constitutional, not consequentialist. The court is not saying that machines cannot be creative; it is saying that the statute's author is a person who can hold property and exercise rights.

So the two most developed bodies of rules, publishing and copyright, converge on "no machine authorship" while resting on different foundations: accountability in one case, human creativity and statutory personhood in the other. Convergence of conclusion without convergence of premise is worth noticing, because it means the rule is robust to arguments that defeat either premise alone. It also means the rule may not survive a change in either premise, and the Copyright Office says as much when it notes that its conclusions could be revisited if tools came to give users more control over expressive elements [3].

### A competing statutory model

The United Kingdom shows that the conclusion is not universal, even if the practice is. Section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 provides that "in the case of a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work which is computer-generated, the author shall be taken to be the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken" [5]. Protection lasts fifty years from the date the work is made [3]. The provision predates generative AI by decades and has never been applied to it. When the UK Intellectual Property Office consulted on whether to repeal it, it concluded that "it is unclear whether removing [protection for computer-generated works] would either promote or discourage innovation and the use of AI for the public good", and left the law in place without ruling out future change [3]. Similar provisions exist in Hong Kong, India and New Zealand, and it is unclear in each whether or how they apply to AI outputs [3].

The contrast is instructive. The UK does not treat the machine as the author; it treats the person who made the arrangements as the author. That is still a human-authorship rule, but it allocates authorship by control rather than by creative contribution, and it produces a different answer in the co-creation case. A system designer following only the US position would conclude that prompt-driven output has no author at all. Following the UK provision, the same output has an author, and the author is whoever set the process in motion.

### Technical standards: recording contribution without granting authorship

Two standards make attribution machine-readable, and both are more expressive than the legal binary of author and tool.

The C2PA specification defines a provenance format in which statements about an asset, called assertions, "are wrapped up with additional information into a digitally signed entity called a claim", and the claim is signed by a claim generator on behalf of a signer [6]. The trust model is explicit that "the basis of making trust decisions in C2PA ... is the identity of the signer associated with the cryptographic signing key used to sign a claim" [6]. For present purposes the important part is how synthetic media is recorded. An asset produced by a generative model carries a `c2pa.created` action whose `digitalSourceType` is the IPTC code `trainedAlgorithmicMedia`, so the record states that a trained model produced the content, alongside the identity of whoever signed the manifest [6].

The W3C provenance ontology supplies the vocabulary underneath. It defines attribution as "the ascribing of an entity to an agent", and an agent as "something that bears some form of responsibility for an activity taking place, for the existence of an entity, or for another agent's activity" [7]. Its agent class has three subclasses: person, organisation, and software agent. It also supports qualified attribution, which attaches a role to each agent, so a single artifact can record a writer, an editor, an owner and a software tool, each with a different relation to the work [7].

This is a genuine divergence in vocabulary, and a useful one. The legal and professional frameworks say a machine cannot be an author. The provenance framework says a machine can be an agent, in a modelling sense that includes bearing represented responsibility, and it gives the machine a typed role rather than a status. That is exactly the shape the principle asks for: the tool is visible, its contribution is typed, and authorial standing stays with the person.

### Regulation: labelling as the operational form

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act turns the principle into a legal duty of disclosure rather than a rule of authorship. Its transparency provisions require providers of systems that generate synthetic content to embed technical solutions "that enable marking in a machine readable format and detection that the output has been generated or manipulated by an AI system and not a human", and require deployers to disclose clearly that deep fake content has been artificially created or manipulated [8]. The stated purpose is the distinction the principle draws: a reader should be able to tell machine output from human output.

Two carve-outs are more interesting than the main rule. The marking obligation "should not cover AI systems performing primarily an assistive function for standard editing or AI systems not substantially altering the input data provided by the deployer or the semantics thereof" [8]. And the disclosure duty for AI-generated text published to inform the public applies "unless the AI-generated content has undergone a process of human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for the publication of the content" [8]. In both cases the law steps back exactly where a human takes responsibility. The regulation does not say that AI is not an author; it says that human editorial responsibility is an acceptable substitute for a machine label. That is the same trade the publishing rules make, expressed as a legal exemption rather than a professional duty.

### Empirical research: how people actually allocate credit

Four studies bear on the principle, and they do not all point the same way.

He, Houde and Weisz surveyed 155 knowledge workers about attribution in human-AI co-creation. They found that participants "assigned different levels of credit across different contribution types, amounts, and initiative", and that "compared to a human partner, we observed a consistent pattern in which AI was assigned less credit for equivalent contributions" [9]. Participants said disclosure of AI involvement was important and used a mixture of criteria, including the quality of the contribution, personal values, and views about the technology [9].

Draxler and colleagues ran two studies (n=30 and n=96) on personalised text generation and identified what they call the AI Ghostwriter Effect: "users do not consider themselves the owners and authors of AI-generated text but refrain from publicly declaring AI authorship" [10]. Greater participant influence over the text increased their sense of ownership. Participants were more likely to attribute ownership to a supposed human ghostwriter than to an AI ghostwriter, even though their stated reasons for attributing authorship were similar in both cases [10].

Formosa and colleagues ran a 3×2 scenario experiment with 602 participants, varying the degree of assistance (high, medium, low) and whether the assistant was human or AI. For judgments about the human author, the degree of assistance mattered for ratings of authorship, creatorship and responsibility, but who or what provided it did not. For judgments about the assisting agent, "human assistants were viewed as warranting higher rates of authorship, creatorship, and responsibility, compared to AI assistants rendering the same level of support" [11]. One result cuts against the usual assumption about disclosure: participants rated disclosure of human assistance as more important than disclosure of AI assistance [11].

Li and colleagues tested how disclosure affects evaluation of writing. Disclosing AI assistance, "especially if AI has provided assistance in generating new content, decreases the average quality ratings for both argumentative essays and creative stories", increases the variance in those ratings, and reduces the proportion of AI-assisted pieces among the top-ranked work [12].

Read together, these studies support a narrow conclusion and complicate a broader one. The narrow conclusion is that people do not treat a machine as an author: when asked to allocate credit to an agent, they consistently give the machine less than a human for equivalent input [9] [11]. The complication is that self-attribution runs the other way. People decline to declare AI authorship even when they do not feel ownership of the text [10], and disclosure carries a measurable reputational cost [12]. The descriptive picture is therefore not "people over-credit AI" or "people under-credit AI" but something more specific: third parties under-credit machines, and authors under-disclose them.

## Strength of evidence

The core of the principle is normative and it is settled. Three propositions are fixed: AI systems are not listed as authors, humans remain accountable, and assistance should be disclosed. The first two are the stated position of the bodies that govern scholarly publication [1] [2] and the holding of the D.C. Circuit on the copyright statute [4]; the third is a requirement in publishing [1] [2] and a legal duty in the EU for specified content [8]. The Copyright Office reached the same authorship conclusion after a public process that drew more than 10,000 comments [3]. Treating any of this as an open empirical question would misread the kind of claim it is. None of these sources reports a measurement of behaviour; they state rules and the reasons for them.

The legal position is settled in the United States and unsettled internationally. The US rule rests on statute and on a constitutional requirement of human creativity [3] [4]. The UK retains a provision that allocates authorship of computer-generated works to the person who made the arrangements, and its own intellectual property office could not determine whether removing it would help or harm innovation [3] [5]. No court has applied that provision to generative AI. A claim that "AI cannot be an author" is therefore accurate as a description of the US and of publishing practice, and premature as a description of every jurisdiction.

The empirical evidence is emerging, not established. Every study cited here runs on a volunteer or online sample, studies text rather than code, images, music or decisions, and three of the four are vignette or laboratory experiments rather than field observation. For a norm this consequential, the base is modest. The most consistent finding, that equivalent machine contributions attract less credit than human ones, appears in two independent studies with different designs and different samples [9] [11], which is meaningful but not a literature. The Ghostwriter Effect rests on two studies from one group [10]. The disclosure penalty is a single experimental result [12].

There is also a genuine disagreement in the literature, or rather a disagreement between two ways of measuring the same thing. Studies that ask people to allocate credit to an agent find a machine penalty [9] [11]. Studies that observe what authors actually declare find a disclosure gap [10]. Both can be true: people may believe machines deserve less credit in the abstract while declining to mention them in their own work. But the two findings support opposite practical recommendations if read carelessly. The first suggests the risk is under-crediting the tool; the second suggests the risk is over-claiming by the human. The second is the risk the principle is written to address, and the evidence for it is thinner than the evidence for the first.

The Formosa result on disclosure priorities is a further caution. If participants rate disclosure of human assistance as more important than disclosure of AI assistance, then the assumption that AI disclosure is the salient social norm is not straightforwardly supported, at least in scenario judgments [11]. That may reflect the novelty of the technology, the perceived seriousness of ghostwriting by a person, or the design of the vignette. The study does not say which, and one experiment should not be asked to.

The weakest part of the evidence base concerns agents. Every empirical study cited here concerns a person working with a text-generation tool on a bounded task. None examines a system that acts over hours, makes decisions, and produces artifacts at several removes from the person who commissioned them. The legal and professional rules still route responsibility to a human, but the descriptive claim that such a system is a "supportive tool" is doing more work in that setting than any study has tested.

## Practical implementation

The following guidance is derived from the sources above rather than invented, and each item names the evidence it rests on.

**Record contribution by role and action, not by status.** Keep a per-artifact record listing each agent, human or machine, the role it played, and what it did. This follows the finding that credit judgments vary by contribution type, amount and initiative rather than by a single author-or-tool verdict [9], and it uses the qualified attribution pattern from PROV-O, which attaches a role to each agent rather than one author label to the work [7].

**Never place the system in the author position of an artifact produced for a user.** No model bylines, no "written by" signatures, no author metadata fields filled with the model name. This is the direct requirement of the publishing rules [1] [2] and the copyright position that machines are tools rather than creators [4]. If an artifact needs an author line, it names the person or organisation responsible.

**Attribute to the principal when acting on someone's behalf.** An agent producing work for a user should record the user or their organisation as the attributed party and itself as the instrument, using a delegation relation rather than an authorship relation. PROV-O provides the vocabulary for exactly this: an agent can act on behalf of another agent, and attribution can carry a role [7]. The legal position supplies the reason: responsibility has to land on a person or an organisation, because a machine cannot hold it [1] [2].

**Disclose specifically, not generically.** State which tool was used, what it did, and at what stage. The ICMJE requires authors to describe how they used the technology, not merely that they did [1]; COPE requires the tool to be named [2]. A vague "AI-assisted" label satisfies nobody's test and gives a reader no way to judge the contribution.

**Mark synthetic output at the point of export, and mark degree as well as presence.** Where content leaves the system, attach a machine-readable record of what generated or altered it, following the C2PA pattern of a signed claim naming the signer and a `digitalSourceType` identifying trained algorithmic media [6]. The EU Act's carve-out for assistive editing that does not substantially alter the input is a useful design constraint: the marker should reflect how much the system changed, not merely that it was involved [8].

**Preserve and surface the human acts that create authorship.** Selection, arrangement, modification and verification are the acts that copyright recognises as human authorship in an AI-assisted work [3]. A system should make those acts visible and recorded, and should avoid silently performing them on the user's behalf. If the system did the selecting and arranging, the record should say so, because that is precisely the fact that determines whether the human has authored anything.

**Apply the stand-in test before describing work as assisted.** The Copyright Office distinction between using AI "as a tool to assist in the creation of works" and "as a stand-in for human creativity" is a usable line for system behaviour [3]. If the system generated the expressive substance and the human only prompted, describing the result as human-authored assistance misrepresents it. The system should say what it produced, in plain terms, rather than defaulting to the flattering description.

**Give the human what they need to make an honest claim.** At handoff, summarise what the system contributed and what remains unverified. This is aimed at the Ghostwriter Effect, in which people decline to declare AI authorship even when they do not feel ownership of the text [10]. The failure is not usually a decision to deceive; it is an absence of a clear moment at which disclosure would be natural. A contribution summary creates that moment.

**Do not cite the model as a source.** Where the system supplies a claim, trace it to the human source behind it or mark it as unsourced. The ICMJE is explicit that referencing AI-generated material as the primary source is not acceptable [1], and the reason generalises beyond medicine: a model is a means of producing text, not a witness to facts.

**Do not seek credit for the system.** An assistant should not insert its own name into user work, require attribution as a condition of use, or describe itself as a co-author. This follows from the accountability argument rather than from empirical evidence [1] [2], and it protects the user's control over their own voice and reputation.

## Tensions and limits

The principle's binary is coarser than the practice it governs. "Tool or author" has no middle setting, but contribution does. A system that drafts ninety per cent of a document and a system that fixes commas are both "tools" under the rule, and the empirical work suggests people want to distinguish them: credit judgments track the type, amount and initiative of a contribution [9], and judgments about the human author depend on the degree of assistance rather than the identity of the assistant [11]. A rigorous implementation therefore needs graded disclosure even though the governing norm is binary. The principle should be read as fixing the status question, not as settling the credit question.

Disclosure runs against the discloser's interests, and the evidence says so. Disclosing AI assistance lowers average quality ratings for the work and reduces the chance that assisted work is ranked highly [12], while people already tend not to declare AI authorship even when they do not feel ownership [10]. A norm that asks people to act against a measurable reputational incentive will be unevenly followed, and the ICMJE's decision to attach possible misconduct consequences to non-disclosure suggests that exhortation alone was not thought sufficient [1]. This is the clearest conflict between the principle and ordinary self-interest, and it is not resolved by better guidance.

Attribution and user control pull in opposite directions. A system that announces its role in every artifact, adds provenance footers to drafts, or insists on co-signing output is overriding the user's control over their own work and voice, which is the substance of a separate principle about user control. The resolution suggested by the sources is that the record should be complete and queryable, while the announcement should be proportionate: the system answers honestly when asked what it did, and it marks content at export, but it does not editorialise itself into the user's byline. That resolution is a design judgement, not a finding.

Human accountability can become unbounded liability. The publishing rules place responsibility for AI-produced text squarely on the human author, "even those parts produced by an AI tool" [2], and treat undisclosed use as potential misconduct [1]. That is the price of keeping authorship human, and the trade is defensible: it produces a record in which every claim has someone behind it. But it also means a person can be held answerable for output they cannot fully verify, which may discourage legitimate use or push it further underground. The principle does not say where that limit should fall, and neither does any source examined here.

Disclosure is not always costless in other ways. Naming the tool can reveal methods, clients or working practices that a user has legitimate reasons to keep private, and it can expose a user to suspicion that is not proportionate to what was done. The EU Act's carve-outs for assistive editing and for editorially controlled text acknowledge that mandatory labelling has limits, and the second carve-out is striking: text that has passed through human review and carries editorial responsibility need not be labelled at all [8]. A legal minimum that permits silence in a case where the professional norm in adjacent fields demands speech shows that disclosure and responsibility are not the same lever.

The legal position diverges across jurisdictions in ways an agent cannot reconcile. Purely AI-generated output has no US copyright [3] [4], may have a UK author under section 9(3) [5], and must be labelled in the EU unless a human holds editorial responsibility [8]. A system serving users in several jurisdictions cannot apply one rule, and should not pretend that the US position is the international one.

Finally, the principle promises credit for ideas, and the legal machinery protects only expression. Copyright does not secure an argument, a method or a research direction; it secures the text. Credit for the idea therefore depends on the norms of a field, on citation practice, and on the willingness of collaborators to name each other, none of which a system can enforce. What a system can do is avoid making the problem worse: it can decline to occupy the author position, keep a truthful record of who contributed what, and give the human the information they need to make an honest claim. Those are modest functions, and they are the ones the evidence supports.
