# Collective learning without damage

## What the principle addresses

The principle joins two ideas that are usually discussed separately. The first is that knowledge grows through contribution and reuse rather than through isolated effort. The second is that this growth must not damage the environment that supports it. Each idea is intelligible on its own. Together they form a claim about how an AI system should acquire and share knowledge, and the claim is doing more work than it first appears.

"Collective learning" has a technical meaning and a broader one. Technically, it describes training that aggregates data or model updates from many parties: federated learning across devices, collaborative training across organisations, shared training corpora assembled from the web, and, in agent systems, shared retrieval stores and memories that persist across users and sessions. Broadly, it describes the older idea that knowledge is a commons, improved by contribution and degraded by enclosure or contamination. Both meanings matter here, because an AI assistant participates in both: it is trained on a collective corpus, and it may write to a shared knowledge store.

"Without causing damage to the environment" is ambiguous in a way that changes the evidence base. Read ecologically, it concerns energy, carbon, water, materials, and land used to train and run models. Read informationally, it concerns the health of the knowledge environment itself: whether the corpus stays diverse, consented, and free of contamination. The two readings are not rivals. A report that picks one silently will mislead, so this report treats both and marks which is which.

Underneath the wording sit three different kinds of statement, and only some can be tested. That knowledge growth is collective is a descriptive claim about how learning systems work. That it should be safe is a normative claim. That it should not damage the environment is a constraint that is partly measurable and partly a policy choice. The research literature supports narrow, mechanism-level versions of the first and third. It does not test the principle as a package, and the literature reviewed here contains no study that measures "safe collective learning" as a single construct. That gap matters when judging how confidently the principle can be stated.

## Established frameworks and research

### Learning together without pooling the data

Federated learning is the clearest technical realisation of the collective idea. McMahan and colleagues proposed leaving training data on mobile devices and learning a shared model by aggregating locally computed updates, using iterative model averaging. Their experiments across five architectures and four datasets showed the approach is robust to the unbalanced and non-independent data that characterises real deployments, and reduced the communication rounds needed by 10 to 100 times compared with synchronised stochastic gradient descent [8]. The contribution is a mechanism, not a promise: it shows that a shared model can improve from many parties' data without that data being centralised.

Privacy is the reason the mechanism matters, and differential privacy is the reason it can be deployed. Abadi and colleagues developed differentially private stochastic gradient descent and showed that deep networks with non-convex objectives can be trained under a modest privacy budget at a manageable cost in training efficiency and model quality [9]. The guarantee is formal, which is what distinguishes it from anonymisation. The cost is real and should not be hidden: privacy is bought with noise, and the noise buys a measurable loss in accuracy.

### Open participation creates an integrity problem

The same openness that makes collective learning valuable makes it attackable. Bagdasaryan and colleagues showed that any participant in federated learning can introduce hidden backdoor functionality into the joint global model, for example causing a classifier to assign an attacker-chosen label to images with particular features. Their model-replacement attack let a single participant, acting in one round, drive the global model to 100 per cent accuracy on the backdoor task. It outperformed data poisoning, and their constrain-and-scale technique incorporated evasion of anomaly-detection defences into the attacker's training objective [10].

The finding generalises into a design principle rather than a narrow result: the more parties can contribute to a shared model or knowledge store, the more the integrity of that store depends on assumptions about contributors. Defences that inspect updates for strangeness can be adapted to. That does not make collective learning unworkable. It means trust cannot be assumed from participation.

### The knowledge commons is neither free nor infinite

Two empirical results complicate the assumption that the web is a standing reserve of training data. The first concerns consent. Longpre and colleagues conducted a longitudinal audit of the consent protocols of 14,000 web domains underlying AI training corpora. They found a rapid proliferation of AI-specific restrictions between 2023 and 2024, inconsistencies between what sites said in their terms of service and what their robots.txt files allowed, and, within a single year, full restrictions covering more than 5 per cent of all tokens in C4 and more than 28 per cent of the most actively maintained critical sources in that corpus. Counting terms-of-service restrictions, 45 per cent of C4 was restricted. The authors describe these as symptoms of web protocols that were not designed for the widespread repurposing of the internet for AI, and warn that if the restrictions are respected or enforced they will bias the diversity, freshness, and scaling of general-purpose systems [11].

The second concerns contamination. Shumailov and colleagues showed in a peer-reviewed Nature paper that training generative models on data produced by previous generations causes model collapse, a degenerative process in which models forget the true underlying distribution. Tails of the distribution disappear first, then variance collapses toward a point estimate. They demonstrated the effect in Gaussian mixture models, variational autoencoders, and language models, and argued it is inevitable under recursive training even with near-ideal conditions. In their language-model experiments, discarding all original data degraded performance across generations, while preserving 10 per cent of the original data led to only minor degradation [12].

Read together, these two papers describe a commons with boundaries and a commons with a maintenance cost. Consent signals are tightening at the same moment that the supply of uncontaminated human text is becoming more valuable. The phrase "increasingly valuable" is the authors' own: they note that data about genuine human interactions with systems will matter more as model-generated content spreads [12].

### The ecological footprint is measurable, at least in part

At the level of the electricity system, the International Energy Agency estimates that data centres accounted for around 1.5 per cent of global electricity consumption in 2024, or 415 terawatt-hours, and projects consumption more than doubling to around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, slightly more than Japan's total consumption today. Its base case reaches around 1,200 terawatt-hours by 2035, with a range of 700 to 1,700 terawatt-hours across its scenarios. Emissions from data-centre electricity use grow from about 180 million tonnes of CO2 today to 300 million tonnes by 2035 in the base case, remaining below 1.5 per cent of total energy-sector emissions but among the fastest-growing sources [5]. The same report argues that broad adoption of existing AI applications could reduce emissions by an amount equivalent to around 5 per cent of energy-related emissions in 2035, larger than data-centre emissions, while cautioning that rebound effects could undercut the gains [5].

At the level of a single model, Luccioni and colleagues estimated the life-cycle carbon footprint of BLOOM, a 176-billion-parameter language model. They put final training at approximately 24.7 tonnes of CO2 equivalent when counting only dynamic power consumption, and 50.5 tonnes when accounting for equipment manufacturing and operational energy together. They also measured the energy and emissions of deployment for inference through an API endpoint receiving real-time queries, and concluded with a discussion of how difficult precise estimation remains [6].

At the level of inference, Luccioni, Jernite, and Strubell compared the ongoing cost of running models rather than training them, measuring energy and carbon per 1,000 inferences for task-specific and general-purpose systems. They found multi-purpose generative architectures orders of magnitude more expensive than task-specific systems for a variety of tasks, even when controlling for the number of parameters, and argued that the utility of general-purpose deployment should be weighed more deliberately against its energy and emissions [7]. For an assistant that answers many small requests, this is the most directly relevant measurement among the sources reviewed here, because it locates cost in the repeated call rather than the one-off training run.

NIST's Generative AI Profile places these findings in a risk-management frame. It lists environmental impacts, defined as impacts due to high compute utilisation in training or operating generative models and related outcomes that may adversely affect ecosystems, as one of twelve named risks, and classifies environmental risk among ecosystem and societal risks rather than model-level malfunctions [4]. Its profile notes that training a single transformer model can emit as much carbon as 300 round-trip flights between San Francisco and New York, that generative tasks were found to be more energy- and carbon-intensive than discriminative tasks, and that distillation or compression can reduce inference impacts while the training and tuning of the smaller model still contributes. It then states the limitation plainly: "Currently there is no agreed upon method to estimate environmental impacts from GAI" [4].

### Governance frameworks

Three frameworks address the principle directly, and all three are normative instruments rather than evidence of effect.

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, adopted by UNESCO's General Conference in 2021, sets out environmental and ecosystem flourishing as a principle to be recognised, protected, and promoted through the AI system life cycle, and describes the environment and ecosystems as the existential necessity that allows anyone to enjoy the benefits of AI [1]. Its policy area on environment and ecosystems asks states and business enterprises to assess direct and indirect environmental impact across the life cycle, including carbon footprint, energy consumption, and the environmental impact of raw material extraction, and to reduce the impact of AI systems and data infrastructures. It also asks states to ensure that AI actors favour data, energy, and resource-efficient methods in line with proportionality, and states that where negative environmental impacts are disproportionate, AI should not be used [1]. That last clause goes beyond a mitigation duty: it is a precautionary stop.

The EU AI Act approaches the same subject through disclosure. Providers of general-purpose models must draw up technical documentation containing, among other things, the computational resources used to train the model and the "known or estimated energy consumption of the model", with the allowance that where consumption is unknown it may be based on information about computational resources used [3]. The same article requires a policy to comply with EU copyright law, including identifying and complying with reservations of rights expressed by rights holders, and a publicly available and sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training [2]. Two features of the design matter. The energy documentation goes to the AI Office and national authorities on request rather than to the public, and the training-content summary is public while the surrounding duties are expressly made without prejudice to the protection of trade secrets and confidential business information [2] [3].

NIST supplies the operational layer that the other two lack. Its suggested actions for environmental impact include documenting anticipated impacts of model development, maintenance, and deployment in product design decisions; measuring or estimating energy and water consumption for training, fine-tuning, and deployment while verifying trade-offs between resources used at inference time and additional resources required at training time; and verifying the effectiveness of carbon capture or offset programmes and addressing green-washing concerns [4].

## Strength of evidence

The mechanism-level claims are empirically well established. Federated learning works under the unbalanced and non-independent data distributions that characterise real deployments [8]. Differential privacy provides a formal guarantee and can be trained into deep networks at a bounded cost [9]. Poisoning of aggregated models is demonstrated, not hypothesised, and the specific attack described achieved its effect in a single round [10]. Measurement of training energy and carbon is mature enough to produce auditable life-cycle estimates for a named model [6], and the finding that generative systems cost orders of magnitude more per inference than task-specific ones is a direct measurement rather than a projection [7].

The system-level environmental claims are emerging and contested. The IEA's figures are among the most authoritative available, and they are explicitly scenario-dependent: a range of 700 to 1,700 terawatt-hours in 2035 is a factor of more than two, which is an honest statement of uncertainty rather than a prediction [5]. Definitions differ across studies, so numbers are not directly comparable. Operational energy and life-cycle energy differ by roughly a factor of two in the BLOOM estimate [6]. Accounting choices matter as much as measurements: attributing emissions by location or by purchased market instruments changes the answer for the same electricity, and whether embodied emissions from hardware manufacturing are counted, and how they are allocated across models trained on shared equipment, is unresolved. NIST's statement that there is no agreed method is the fairest summary, and it should be quoted whenever a single carbon number is presented as fact [4].

The direction of the net effect is genuinely disputed. The IEA's estimate that AI applications could reduce emissions by around 5 per cent of energy-related emissions in 2035, larger than the emissions attributable to data centres, sits alongside its warning that rebound effects could erode those gains [5]. This is not a case where one side has the evidence. It is a case where the answer depends on which applications are adopted, how quickly, and what they displace.

The consent findings are measured but narrow. The audit covers 14,000 domains and one year, and it measures expressed preferences rather than compliance or legal effect [11]. It establishes that restrictions are proliferating and inconsistently encoded. It does not establish that training data has become unavailable, because enforcement, licensing deals, and synthetic data all change the picture.

Model collapse is well established as a laboratory phenomenon and contested as a forecast. The mathematical argument for discrete distributions is a proof, and the language-model experiments show degradation under recursive fine-tuning [12]. The extrapolation to frontier training runs is less secure, because those runs are not trained purely on a predecessor's output. The paper's own mitigation result, that retaining 10 per cent of the original data limited degradation to minor levels, is the most useful part of the finding and the least often quoted [12].

The governance frameworks are normative and prescriptive. UNESCO's recommendation creates no enforceable duty, and its precautionary clause describes what states should require rather than what firms do [1]. The AI Act's energy documentation is a reporting duty whose measurement methodology the Commission is still empowered to specify by delegated act [2] [3]. NIST's profile is voluntary guidance [4]. None of these sources shows that disclosure reduces emissions. They show that disclosure is now expected, which is a different claim.

There is no direct evidence for the principle as stated. The literature reviewed here contains no study that tests whether organisations which learn collectively and safely outperform those that do not, and none that measures "damage to the environment" as a combined ecological and epistemic variable. The principle is a synthesis of well-supported components and a normative commitment. That is a reasonable thing for a checklist to contain, provided it is not presented as an empirical finding.

## Practical implementation

The guidance below follows from the sources rather than from general good practice. Each item names the finding it rests on.

**Measure the right thing, and state the method.** Track energy or carbon per request where the platform exposes it, and record whether the figure is operational or life-cycle, and location-based or market-based. NIST's suggested action is to measure or estimate impacts for training, fine-tuning, and deployment and to verify the trade-off between inference-time and training-time resources [4]. Since no agreed method exists [4], the honest output is a number plus its scope, not a number alone.

**Route by task difficulty.** One of the largest levers an assistant controls is which model answers which request. Generative multi-purpose models were orders of magnitude more costly per thousand inferences than task-specific systems for a range of tasks, even controlling for parameter count [7]. Classification, extraction, routing, and short factual lookups should not default to the largest general model when a smaller or fine-tuned one performs adequately.

**Cache, batch, and constrain context.** Repeated identical or near-identical requests should be served from a cache. Long contexts and multi-step agent loops multiply inference cost, and the measured cost is per inference, so loop depth is an environmental variable, not only a latency one [7]. Deferrable batch work can be placed where and when electricity is cleaner, because operational emissions depend on the generating mix supplying the data centre; that scheduling intervention is not tested in the sources reviewed here, so it should be described as reasoning from the measurement literature rather than as an evaluated practice.

**Distil for volume, but count the training cost.** Compression and distillation reduce inference impact, and NIST notes that training and tuning the smaller model still contributes to the total [4]. The comparison to make is whole-life cost for the expected request volume, not the cost of a single call.

**Treat the training corpus as consented material.** Record provenance, honour robots.txt and terms-of-service reservations, and keep a record of what was excluded and why. The AI Act's requirement of a copyright-compliance policy that identifies reservations of rights, and a public summary of training content, is a usable template even where the Act does not apply [2]. The audit evidence that restrictions are proliferating and inconsistently encoded [11] means a provenance record will age; it needs a review date, not a one-time sign-off.

**Keep real data and label synthetic data.** Retaining a fraction of original human-generated data substantially limited degradation in the model-collapse experiments [12]. In practice: do not train or fine-tune on your own outputs without a retained real-data anchor, label synthetic content at the point of creation, and treat the presence of model-generated text in a corpus as a data-quality fact that needs recording.

**Apply privacy at the point of aggregation.** Where learning draws on user interactions, aggregate rather than centralise where possible [8], and apply a differential-privacy mechanism with a stated budget where the output could reveal individual records [9]. Do not place personal data in a shared memory or retrieval store that other users' sessions can reach, a leakage risk NIST names directly [4]. The privacy budget is a design parameter that should be set deliberately, because it trades directly against model quality.

**Treat shared knowledge stores as untrusted input.** Contributions to a shared model can carry deliberate corruption, and the demonstrated attack needed one participating round to take effect [10]. Validate contributions against independent sources, limit what any single contributor can shift, and do not rely on anomaly detection alone, since the published attack was designed to evade it.

**Report environmental impact in design decisions, and avoid unearned claims.** NIST recommends documenting anticipated environmental impacts in product design decisions and verifying offset or carbon-capture claims to address green-washing [4]. A system that reports a carbon figure without its scope, or an offset without evidence of additionality, is making a claim it cannot support.

**Prefer efficiency as a constraint, not a slogan.** UNESCO's policy area asks actors to favour data, energy, and resource-efficient methods in line with proportionality, and to forgo AI where environmental impacts are disproportionate [1]. Operationally, that means setting a cost budget per task class before choosing a model, rather than optimising afterwards.

## Tensions and limits

The principle conflicts with privacy in a way that cannot be fully resolved. Collective learning is valuable precisely because it draws on many people's data, and privacy protection limits what can be drawn. Federated learning reduces centralisation but does not by itself provide a formal guarantee [8], and differential privacy provides the guarantee at a measurable cost in quality [9]. Any system that claims both full privacy and unrestricted collective learning is overstating one of them.

It conflicts with transparency in the other direction. The AI Act's energy documentation is provided to regulators on request, and its transparency duties are expressly conditioned on protecting trade secrets and confidential business information [2] [3]. Public accountability and commercial confidentiality are being traded against each other in the text of the regulation itself. An assistant asked to disclose its training data or its energy use should describe what is documented and for whom, rather than implying that public disclosure is complete.

It conflicts with helpfulness. Routing to a smaller model, caching an answer, or refusing a request on environmental grounds all reduce capability or freshness in some cases. The evidence supports the efficiency gain [7]; it does not establish that the quality loss is acceptable in any particular domain, and that judgement belongs to the deployer, not the model.

It conflicts with user control. A user's interactions improving a shared model is a collective benefit that the individual did not necessarily choose. The consent audit [11] and the privacy mechanisms [8] [9] give partial answers, but the underlying question, whether contribution should be opt-in or opt-out, is a policy choice that the research does not settle.

It conflicts with openness in safety. Publishing detailed safety findings and incident data helps collective defence, and publishing capability-relevant detail helps misuse. NIST's risk taxonomy treats information integrity and information security as misuse risks in their own right [4], which is a reminder that the knowledge commons can be damaged by well-intentioned disclosure as well as by contamination.

The measurement base is thin in specific places. There is no agreed method for estimating generative AI's environmental impact [4]; system-level projections span a factor of more than two [5]; inference costs are measured for a small number of models and tasks [7]; the consent audit covers one year and expressed preferences rather than outcomes [11]; and the collapse experiments use small models and fine-tuning rather than frontier-scale pre-training [12]. Claims about the net environmental benefit of AI rest on scenario modelling, not observation [5].

The composite has no direct evidence. The principle is a conjunction of a design claim, a safety requirement, and an environmental constraint. Each component has support; the conjunction has not been tested, and a checklist that presents it as established would be claiming more than the literature shows.

A final ambiguity deserves stating rather than resolving. If "environment" means the natural environment, the relevant evidence is energy, carbon, water, and materials, and the implementation levers are model choice, routing, and reporting. If it means the knowledge environment, the relevant evidence is consent, contamination, and integrity, and the levers are provenance, data hygiene, and validation. A checklist entry that does not say which it means will be read as covering both and implemented as neither.
