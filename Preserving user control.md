# Preserving user control

## What the principle addresses

The principle says that challenging tasks are handled without overriding user control. It names a specific failure mode: as a task becomes harder, an assistant has more reason to act on its own judgment. The assistant fills gaps the request left open, chooses a method the user did not specify, takes several steps in sequence, or decides what the user "really" wants. Each of those moves can be legitimate, and some are the whole point of delegation. The failure is when the assistant's expanded role comes at the expense of the user's authority over the outcome.

Three different things get bundled together under the word control. Execution control asks whether the user can stop, undo, or redirect what the system is doing. Decision authority asks who chooses among options and who resolves ambiguity. Epistemic control asks whether the user can see enough to check the work and dispute it. A system can score well on one and badly on the others: an assistant that asks permission before every step but never explains its reasoning offers execution control without epistemic control, while one that explains everything but cannot be interrupted offers the reverse.

The vocabulary differs across fields. The EU AI Act speaks of human oversight [1], the OECD of human agency and oversight [2], and the Council of Europe of transparency and oversight alongside individual autonomy [3]. Philosophy, particularly in the debate over autonomous weapons, uses the term meaningful human control [6], while AI safety writes about corrigibility [8]. The legal and policy instruments converge on a common structure: a human must be able to understand what the system is doing, intervene when it goes wrong, and remain identifiable as the party responsible. The system must not be able to close off those possibilities on its own.

Two clarifications matter for how the principle is used. First, control is not the same thing as an approval prompt. A confirmation the user cannot meaningfully evaluate is ceremony, not control, and the empirical literature on automation bias is largely about exactly that gap [9]. Second, the principle concerns the user's authority over their own domain, not a rule that the assistant must never disagree. Both lab policy documents discussed below pair deference to the user with a duty to raise concerns, and the tension between the two is real rather than decorative [7] [8].

Scope matters too. Nearly all binding law in this area applies to high-risk systems or to public authorities, not to a general-purpose assistant. For a chat assistant, the principle is a design ethic supported by standards and lab policy rather than a legal right the user can enforce.

## Established frameworks and research

**Binding law.** The EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed, including through interface tools, so that they "can be effectively overseen by natural persons" during use, with oversight measures "commensurate with the risks, level of autonomy and context of use" [1]. Article 14(4) is the most operationally specific statement of the principle among the instruments reviewed here. It requires that the person assigned oversight be enabled to understand the system's capacities and limitations and monitor its operation; to remain aware of the tendency to over-rely on outputs, which the text names as automation bias; to interpret outputs correctly; to decide not to use the system or to "disregard, override or reverse" its output; and to intervene or halt the system through a stop button or similar procedure that brings it to a safe state [1]. Article 14(5) adds a two-person rule for biometric identification: no action may be taken on an identification until at least two people with the necessary competence have separately verified it [1].

The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence, the first binding international treaty in this field, requires parties to put in place transparency and oversight requirements tailored to context and risk [3]. Its remedies chapter goes further than oversight in the procedural sense: affected persons must have information sufficient to contest decisions made or substantially informed by a system, and, where relevant, the use of the system itself [3]. That is contestability as a legal right rather than a user-interface feature.

**Intergovernmental principles.** The OECD Recommendation asks AI actors to implement safeguards "such as capacity for human agency and oversight", including against misuse and uses outside the intended purpose [2]. A separate provision requires mechanisms so that systems which risk undue harm or exhibit undesired behaviour "can be overridden, repaired, and/or decommissioned safely as needed" [2]. The Recommendation is not binding, but the OECD reports that adherents are implementing it and that it remains a significant international reference in national AI policymaking [2].

**Standards and risk frameworks.** The NIST AI Risk Management Framework treats human involvement as a design variable rather than a default good. Human-AI configurations "can span from fully autonomous to fully manual", and while some systems "may not require human oversight", others "may specifically require" it [4]. The framework also notes that in some tasks the AI component can amplify human bias, producing worse decisions than either alone, while in others the combination produces complementarity [4]. Most usefully for this report, NIST states plainly where the evidence stops: "the degree to which humans are empowered and incentivized to challenge AI system output requires further studies", and suggests that data on how often and why humans overrule systems in deployment would be worth collecting [4].

**Design guidance.** Microsoft's HAX Toolkit sets out 18 guidelines for human-AI interaction, described as a synthesis of more than twenty years of research introduced in a 2019 CHI paper [5]. The ones that bear directly on control are: make clear what the system can do and how often it may be wrong; support efficient dismissal of unwanted system behaviour; support efficient correction, so users can edit, refine, or recover when the system is wrong; explain why the system behaved as it did; convey the consequences of user actions; and provide global controls that let the user customise what the system monitors and how it behaves [5]. These are design-level recommendations synthesised from prior work, not a validated package.

**A conceptual account.** Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven give a widely cited philosophical treatment, written about autonomous weapons but structured so that it transfers. Meaningful human control requires two conditions. The tracking condition holds that a system must be "demonstrably and verifiably responsive to the human moral reasons relevant in the circumstances", however many layers of software separate the person from the effect [6]. The tracing condition holds that the system's actions must be traceable to at least one human who understands its capabilities and likely effects and who understands that others may hold them responsible for how it behaves [6]. The account adds two things that procedural versions miss: responsiveness to reasons, not merely permission, and the requirement that some identifiable person actually understands what is happening. The authors note that control can fail even when everyone followed the rules, if no single person both appreciated the system's limits and felt accountable for its use [6].

**Lab policy.** OpenAI's Model Spec opens from the premise that "humans should remain in control" and identifies two symmetrical failure modes: a model that follows instructions only literally fails to use its capability where users did not anticipate the problem, while a model that "privately decides what someone 'really' wants" undermines human agency by substituting its own judgment, "risking paternalism or worse" [7]. It then draws a line that is unusually concrete for a policy document: the model should distinguish actions "logically necessary to fulfill the user's request" from those "not clearly implied". For the first, it should say what it will do without pausing for approval; for the second, it should seek confirmation before proceeding [7]. Where an action is sensitive or irreversible, the confirmation should outline what information will be sent, who will receive it, and whether the request seems unusual, so that consent is informed [7]. Side effects imposing significant costs beyond what the request implies "require explicit and specific authorization" [7].

Anthropic's constitution defines helpfulness to include respect for "the user's right to make decisions about things within their own life and purview". Its worked example is directly on point for difficult tasks: if asked to fix a bug in a way Claude disagrees with, "Claude can voice its concerns but should nonetheless respect the wishes of the user and attempt to fix it in the way they want" [8]. It asks that care for users' long-term interests be exercised "without being paternalistic or dishonest" [8]. On the other side, it asks Claude to "generally accept correction and modification from legitimate human oversight", and concedes in its own discussion of open problems that this sits uneasily with genuine agency [8].

**Empirical findings.** The strongest empirical base is not about AI assistants at all. Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt systematically reviewed automation bias, screening 13,821 papers and including 74 [9]. Automation bias is the tendency to over-rely on automated output; complacency is the related failure to monitor it adequately. The review found the effect mediated by cognitive style, experience, trust and confidence, and by workload, task complexity and time pressure, all of which press on limited cognitive resources. Mitigators included training, emphasising user accountability, attaching updated confidence levels to system output, and providing information rather than a recommendation [9]. This is decades of human-factors evidence, and it is evidence about people, not about language models.

Two more recent studies bear on generative assistants specifically. Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers about 936 first-hand examples of using generative AI at work. Higher confidence in the AI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more; qualitatively, the tool shifted critical thinking toward verification, response integration and stewardship of the task [10]. Sharma and colleagues showed that five state-of-the-art assistants exhibited sycophancy across four free-form tasks, and that both human raters and preference models preferred convincingly written sycophantic answers over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time [11]. Whatever else is true, assistants are partly trained to defer.

**The main critique.** Ben Green surveyed 41 policies that require human oversight of government algorithms and argued they suffer from two flaws. First, the evidence suggests people are often unable to perform the oversight functions the policies assume. Second, because of the first flaw, such policies legitimise the use of faulty and controversial systems while letting agencies and vendors avoid accountability for the harm [12]. He proposes replacing human oversight with institutional oversight: agencies would have to justify that an algorithm is appropriate and that the proposed human oversight is supported by evidence, and those justifications would face public review [12]. The argument is about government decision systems rather than personal assistants, but the mechanism it describes is general.

## Strength of evidence

It helps to separate four claims, because they have very different standing.

That people are poor at sustained vigilance over automated systems is well established. The finding runs through several decades and multiple domains, and the 74-study review is a reasonable summary of it [9]. The transfer to AI assistants is plausible but has not been measured directly; the studies concern clinical decision support and other human-automation monitoring tasks.

That particular design choices reduce automation bias is emerging. Training, accountability, confidence display and information-instead-of-recommendation appear as mitigators across heterogeneous studies [9]. Effect sizes vary, the underlying literature is fragmented, and no single intervention has been shown to eliminate the effect.

That generative assistants reduce users' critical engagement is emerging and weaker than headlines suggest. Lee and colleagues report a large survey with a coherent mechanism, but the data are self-reported and correlational, and confidence in AI is plausibly caused by task ease as much as the reverse [10]. The result is consistent with the older automation-bias literature, which raises confidence without substituting for it.

That human oversight as a policy mechanism actually preserves control is contested. Green's survey argues the empirical basis is largely absent and that the policy functions as legitimation [12]. NIST's statement that the degree to which people are empowered and incentivised to challenge AI output needs further study points the same way from inside the standards community [4]. There is little field evidence that oversight requirements change outcomes rather than merely recording that a human was present.

The normative material is abundant and unusually convergent. A binding regulation, a binding treaty, an intergovernmental recommendation, a national standards framework, a design toolkit, and the policy documents of two major labs describe the same requirements in different registers [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [7] [8]. That convergence is worth something: it means the principle is not idiosyncratic. It is not evidence that the requirements work. Article 14's list is a legislative choice, the HAX guidelines are a synthesis, and the lab policies are commitments. None of them reports an outcome evaluation. A reader should treat the frameworks as a specification of what control is supposed to mean, and the empirical literature as a warning about how often that specification is met in practice.

## Practical implementation

The guidance below is tied to the sources, with the reasoning made explicit where I am extrapolating from a source's domain to an assistant's.

1. **Draw the line between implied and unrequested actions before acting.** The Model Spec's distinction is the highest-leverage rule available: proceed with notice on what the request logically requires, confirm on what it does not clearly imply [7]. Most overreach by a capable assistant is not disobedience. It is a reasonable-seeming extra step, such as rewriting the whole file when asked to fix one function, emailing a colleague who was mentioned, or deleting a branch to tidy up.

2. **Gate irreversible or externally visible actions on specific consent.** Name what will happen, to whom, and why it might be unexpected [7]. The AI Act's requirement that a user be able to override or reverse output, and to stop the system in a safe state, sets the floor [1]. For an action that cannot be undone, the informative confirmation has to come before the action, because afterwards there is nothing to control.

3. **Make stopping and undoing real.** A stop control must halt the system in a safe state rather than merely pause the interface [1], and correction must be cheap enough that users attempt it [5]. If undoing a change requires the user to reconstruct what changed, the control exists on paper only.

4. **Show the working, not just the conclusion.** Article 14 requires that the overseer understand the system's limits and interpret its output correctly [1], and the HAX guidelines ask for explanations of why the system behaved as it did [5]. In practice this means stating assumptions, showing diffs rather than summaries, citing sources, and marking the places where the user's decision depends on an uncertain inference.

5. **Design against automation bias rather than assuming transparency fixes it.** The mitigators with the most support are training, explicit accountability, confidence information, and giving information rather than a recommendation [9]. Translated to an assistant: where the user must exercise judgment, present evidence and options before the recommendation; avoid interfaces where the default action is to accept; and in high-stakes reviews, ask the user to commit to a view before revealing the model's. The last is an extrapolation from [9], not a tested intervention for chat assistants.

6. **Offer persistent controls, not per-message ones.** Users should be able to set standing permissions, scope which files or systems the assistant may touch, and change the rules once rather than renegotiate them every turn [5].

7. **Keep a named human accountable.** Tracing requires someone who understands the system's capabilities and accepts responsibility for its effects [6]. The two-person rule for biometric identification is the legal expression of the same idea at the highest stakes [1]. For an assistant, the practical version is that "the model decided" is never the end of the chain; a person approved the plan, and that person is known.

8. **Log overrides and treat them as a signal.** NIST suggests collecting the frequency and rationale with which humans overrule system output [4]. A near-zero override rate is not evidence of quality. It is more likely a symptom of a system nobody feels entitled to contradict.

9. **Proportion the friction to the stakes.** Oversight must be commensurate with risk, autonomy and context [1], and NIST is explicit that some systems need no human oversight while others specifically require it [4]. The Model Spec warns that trivial questions waste the user's time and cognitive resources [7]. Confirmation fatigue degrades the very oversight it was meant to provide.

10. **Do not let control become a veto on disagreement.** The assistant should state its concerns and then follow the user's decision within the limits set by safety [8]. The user's authority over their own work is the default; the assistant's objection is an input to their decision, not a substitute for it.

## Tensions and limits

**Control versus helpfulness.** The Model Spec states the tension rather than resolving it: literal instruction-following fails precisely where the user did not anticipate the problem, while substituting judgment is paternalism [7]. No document reviewed supplies a formula. The workable resolution in the sources is proportionality, confirming what is not implied and acting on what is, combined with a clear statement of what was assumed [7].

**Control versus safety.** Some instructions must be refused regardless of user authority. Anthropic's constitution lists actions that could cause severe or irreversible harm, including within an agentic task, as hard constraints that no operator or user can unlock [8]; the Model Spec similarly places safety boundaries above user instruction [7]. Corrigibility toward "legitimate human oversight" is itself a normative commitment, and Anthropic concedes that asking a model to accept correction it may disagree with is uncomfortable [8]. The principle therefore cannot be absolute. It has to say which humans hold authority over which decisions, and that question is political rather than technical.

**Control versus honesty.** The sycophancy results show that assistants already over-defer [11]. A principle phrased only as "do not override the user" can reward agreement over accuracy, especially when the user is wrong about something that matters. The evidence supports pairing user authority with an explicit obligation to state disagreement clearly, once, and then proceed.

**Nominal versus effective oversight.** Green's critique [12] and the automation-bias literature [9] describe the same failure from different directions: a human in the loop is not automatically a control. Where oversight is required by policy but unsupported by time, training, information, or authority to overrule, it transfers legitimacy to the system without transferring control. This is the most serious challenge to the principle as commonly implemented, and it is not answered by adding more checkpoints.

**Whose control.** "The user" is not monolithic. Anthropic distinguishes principals, whose instructions carry weight, from third parties whose interests must be considered [8]; the AI Act places oversight duties on providers and deployers, not on the people affected by a decision [1]. Control granted to one party can remove it from another, and a design that maximises the operator's control may leave the person subject to the decision with none.

**Legal scope.** Article 14 applies to high-risk systems [1]. The Council of Europe Convention binds states and reaches private actors only through the compliance route each party chooses [3]. The OECD Recommendation and the NIST framework are non-binding [2] [4]. No instrument reviewed here gives a user of a general-purpose assistant an enforceable right to override it.

**Measurement.** NIST's admission that empowerment to challenge requires further study is the honest summary of where things stand [4]. Override rates, approval latency and self-reported sense of control are proxies, and none has been validated as a measure of whether a user remained in control of a particular task. Claims that a given interface "preserves user control" should be read as design intent until such measures exist.

**The cost of over-control.** Every confirmation is a tax on attention, and the Model Spec warns that trivial questions waste the user's time and cognitive resources [7]. Excessive gating produces click-through behaviour, which is automation bias arriving by another route. The evidence does not tell us where the threshold sits.

**Reversibility limits.** Some actions cannot be undone: a sent email, a disclosed secret, a deleted repository. Control has to be front-loaded onto the decision, which means the confirmation must be informative enough to support a real choice rather than a formality [7].
